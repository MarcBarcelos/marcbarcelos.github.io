I'm a PhD Fellow at Aarhus University, based at TEXT: Center for Contemporary Cultures of Text, where I'm supervised by Professor Mads Rosendahl Thomsen. My research asks what happens to authorship, style, and reception when people write with large language models like ChatGPT or Claude. Previously, questions regarding who wrote a text, how it reads, and how readers receive it often were easier to answer and more closely linked. However, human–AI co-writing has begun to pull them apart, and it is precisely in these gaps that worries about AI and writing sit, such as questions of stylistic convergence, the loss of expressive diversity, and weakened authorial agency.

My dissertation builds a framework for measuring all three gaps together, on the same texts and the same readers. I am developing a set of interpretable style dimensions for narrative prose. Using factor analysis, I reduce more than 70 stylometric measures (lexical, syntactic, structural, affective, and semantic) to ten interpretable dimensions. I fit them on a reference corpus that runs from canonical novels to flash fiction and fanfiction. Because the reference model is frozen, each dimension keeps its meaning across contexts, linking what stylometry can count with what a close reader would call style. I've shown previously with these tools that fanfiction communities develop distinct styles over time. I have also shown that when a language model continues a writer's story, it follows the writer's style on most dimensions, but within a narrower corridor that pulls toward the average.

This framework will then be applied to human–AI fiction, namely real ChatGPT conversations from WildChat in which people ask for stories. Because every turn is logged, I can measure who wrote which sentences and how the two collaborated. I can also measure how each passage scores on the style dimensions and how it was received. The larger questions are which stylistic qualities read as human, whether human writing is drifting toward LLM defaults, what AI cannot reproduce, and who deserves credit when authorship, style, and reception come apart.

Before my PhD, I earned an M.Sc. in Cognitive Science from Aarhus University and a B.A. in Psychology (magna cum laude) from California State University, Long Beach, and worked as a research assistant at the Center for Music in the Brain and Aarhus Institute of Advanced Studies. I bring that background in cognitive modeling, statistics, and natural language processing to questions from literary studies.
